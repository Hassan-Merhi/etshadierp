Gotcha, thanks for explaining — you’re not making clothes, you’re running a used/original clothing sorting & baling factory. I’ll describe it in your context.

I’ll treat your factory as a “used clothing sorting & baling operation” and show how it’s typically run step by step.

1. Big picture of your business

Input:

Containers of original / credential clothing from:

Europe

Australia

America

These are mixed (all kinds of garments together in bags/sacks).

Inside your factory:

You offload the containers.

Workers open bags and sort items into categories.

Each category is compressed into bales.

Bales are weighed, labeled, and entered on an invoice.

You sell the bales to your customers (wholesalers, shop owners, exporters, etc.).

Output:

Bales like:

Children cotton

Ladies jeans

Men’s T-shirts

Mixed summer

Shoes, Bags, etc.
Each bale has: category + origin + weight + maybe quality.

2. Typical workflow inside your factory
Step 1: Receiving the containers

Container arrives → offload into your warehouse.

Record:

Container number

Supplier (Europe/Australia/USA)

Estimated weight

You might tag them as:

EU–01, AU–02, US–03, etc. so you know later which stock came from where.

Step 2: Opening & rough sorting

You set up sorting tables or conveyor lines.

Workers open the original bags and do a first (rough) sort:

By wearer:

Men

Women

Children

By item type:

T-shirts

Shirts

Trousers/jeans

Dresses/skirts

Jackets/coats

Underwear

By other big groups:

Shoes

Bags

Bedding (sheets, blankets)

Waste (too damaged, dirty, etc.)

At this stage it’s fast sorting, not very detailed. The idea is to get big categories separated.

Step 3: Fine sorting & grading

Now each big category goes for detailed sorting:

Example: Children cotton
Someone will sort children’s clothes by:

Material / season:

Cotton summer

Winter / fleece

Condition (grade) – you can define your own grades, for example:

Cream / Grade A – new or almost new, branded, no stains, no holes

Grade B – good condition, minor wear

Grade C – more wear, for cheaper markets

Wipers / recycling – for cutting into rags
(You may or may not use all these — just an example system.)

Example: Ladies items could be split into:

Ladies jeans

Ladies tops / T-shirts

Ladies dresses

Ladies winter (jackets, cardigans)

Etc.

Each category/grade is collected in its own cage, bin, or trolley.

3. Making the bales

When a bin/trolley is full for a certain category, it goes to the press (baling machine).

Step 1: Loading the press

Load the sorted clothes (e.g. Children cotton summer, Grade A).

Press to your standard bale weight:

e.g. 45 kg, 50 kg, 55 kg, 100 kg – whatever you use.

Step 2: Strapping & labeling

Strap the bale with metal or plastic straps.

Attach a label or write directly on the bale:

Bale code (e.g. KID-COT-SUM-A-001)

Category (Children Cotton Summer)

Grade (A/B/C)

Origin (EU / AUS / USA)

Weight (e.g. 45.3 kg)

Date pressed

All this info should also go into your stock sheet or system.

Step 3: Moving to finished bale area

Finished, labeled bales are moved to a “ready for sale” area.

You might have rows like:

Row 1: Children bales

Row 2: Ladies bales

Row 3: Men’s bales

Row 4: Shoes, bags, bedding, etc.

4. Invoicing and selling to customers

When a customer places an order, you usually sell bales by type, grade, and origin, not piece by piece.

a) Order example

Customer wants:

50 bales – Children cotton mix, EU origin

30 bales – Ladies jeans, AUS origin

20 bales – Men’s T-shirts, USA origin

b) Preparing the invoice

For each bale you include:

Bale code

Description (e.g. “Children cotton mix, EU, Grade B”)

Weight

Price per kg or price per bale

Total per line

Grand total

So your invoice lines might look like:

KID-COT-MIX-EU-045 – Children cotton mix, EU, 45.2 kg × $X/kg

LAD-JEANS-AUS-012 – Ladies jeans, AUS, 55.0 kg × $Y/kg
… and so on.

You can also group them:

50 bales Children cotton mix, EU – total weight 2,250 kg – $X/kg

30 bales Ladies jeans, AUS – total weight 1,650 kg – $Y/kg

c) Loading & delivery

Pick the bales listed on the invoice.

Load onto truck / container.

Customer signs delivery note (and maybe you send packing list + invoice sets).

5. Main roles in this kind of factory

You don’t need sewing operators; your key people are:

Warehouse / receiving team

Offload containers, count bags, keep stock.

Sorters

Do first and second sorting.

Need clear training on categories and quality grades.

Quality controller (QC)

Checks that the category and quality match the label.

Prevents cheating or mistakes (e.g. winter in summer bales, too many damaged pieces).

Press/baling operators

Operate the press safely.

Ensure correct weights and labels.

Stock / inventory person

Records every bale produced.

Tracks what stock is available by type, origin, grade.

Sales / admin

Talks to customers.

Prepares invoices, packing lists, and receipts.

Safety / maintenance

Keeps presses, forklifts, and warehouse safe and working.
